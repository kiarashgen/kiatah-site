About / Kiarash Taheri

I'm Kiarash.

I study architecture, though at this point I'm not entirely sure what that says about what I actually do.

I like making things and figuring out how things work. Sometimes that means buildings, sometimes software, robots, fabrication, experiments, drawings, writing, or something I got curious about for no particularly useful reason.

A little more

I spend a lot of time thinking about people, technology, cities, culture, science, the future, and lately especially AI and what increasingly capable machines might mean for all of us. I don't think I have a neat position on any of it yet. I'm mostly trying to understand things as I go.

I'm from Tehran. Iran, its cities, landscapes, architecture, history and literature are things I keep coming back to, alongside a lot of other places and ideas.

This site is basically a collection of things I've worked on, things I'm working on, and probably eventually some thoughts and other stuff I want to keep somewhere.

The Shahrak Gharb brief began with a site and a housing question. Sketches and Grasshopper experiments asked how households, cores, gardens, light and circulation might affect one another. KIARASH TAHERI / NEUROARCHITECTURE

Studying spatial
experience.

I work on ways to study how people experience architecture, combining research methods, a programmable study workbench, and practical experiments with EEG and VR.

Research assistant to Dr. Bushra Abbasi
RESEARCH FOCUS

How to turn a change in an architectural environment into a meaningful, controlled comparison of experience.

MY CONTRIBUTION

Research methods, NeuroArch software development, and investigation of the practical relationship between EEG and VR.

CURRENT STAGE

A workbench in development, a completed technical pilot, and a controlled spatial study being designed.

01 Question02 Instrument03 Co-wear04 Sequence05 Readout06 Next study
01 / RESEARCH QUESTION

How can an architectural question become a study?

A change in light, geometry or a view needs a clear comparison. My interest is in connecting what we design to what people encounter, report and do, alongside the signals we record.

01Start with a design questionChoose the spatial change and keep the other conditions comparable.
02Record the encounterKeep the scene version, exposure order, timing and equipment state together.
03Interpret the responses togetherConnect reported experience and behavior with physiology before drawing a design conclusion.

These questions guide the study design. Explore the research framework ↗

02 / NEUROARCH WORKBENCH

Building the tools to carry the question through.

I am developing NeuroArch to connect study design, running a session, reviewing recordings and analysing data. It keeps the study version and the original records connected, so an interpretation can be traced back to the conditions that produced it.

Workbench system design

DESIGN → RUN → REVIEW

A sequence through the implemented software, using development test studies.

NeuroArch connects study authoring, event recording, signal review and analysis. Each result stays linked to the study version and recording it came from.

A reproducible analysis check

In an October 2026 software test, NeuroArch froze six generated observations with their original rows and an analysis recipe. The exported reproduction completed successfully and matched the retained summary. This checks the software’s path from source data to a repeatable result; the values are synthetic.

Development evidence: frozen observation analysis and reproduction, 8 October 2026.

03 / PHYSICAL PILOT · 31 AUG 2026

Can EEG and VR work together on the same head?

Before comparing spaces, we need to understand the equipment. A technical pilot on 31 August 2026 examined how wearing a Meta Quest 2 over an EEG cap affected contact and the recorded signal. It used a Mitsar SmartBCI X64 and one participant.

04 / PILOT SEQUENCE

Keep the sequence beside the signal.

The recording moved through eyes-open and eyes-closed baselines, headset wear, two VR scenes and a later movement condition. Keeping that order visible helps explain what each comparison can tell us.

964 snamed condition intervals
884 sselected spectral windows

The conditions were presented once in a fixed order. Physical display onset was not measured, and the final movement block's eye state was unspecified.

05 / WHAT THE PILOT TAUGHT US

From the first recording to a better protocol.

The useful outcome was a set of practical lessons: allow the equipment to settle, check a recognizable physiological response, and separate scene differences from changes in time and participant state.

01 / HEADSET FITTING

Leave time for the headset to settle.

The pilot recording shows a large disturbance around headset fitting, followed by settling. This makes preparation and settling time part of the protocol, before comparing the VR exposures.

02 / BASELINE

Check an eyes-open and eyes-closed baseline.

The eyes-closed and eyes-open comparison showed a recognizable posterior alpha response. It provides a useful check on the recorded physiology before looking at more complicated conditions.

03 / COMPARISON

Repeat the comparison in a balanced order.

The Gray and Valley intervals differ, but they also occurred at different times in the session. Order, eye state, movement and headset contact must be controlled before a difference can be attributed to the environment.

SCOPE OF THIS PILOTThese figures describe one participant in one fixed sequence. They guide the next protocol; they do not establish how an architectural feature affects a population.
06 / NEXT STUDY · IN DEVELOPMENT

Return to the architectural question.

The next step is a controlled comparison of a defined spatial change. I want the design of the environment, the participant’s experience and the analysis to remain connected through the whole study.

01A defined spatial changeChoose one variable and hold the base environment and route steady.
02Balanced orderDistribute conditions across positions and repeat baseline checks.

STRUCTURAL KIT / DESIGN + PRINTING + ASSEMBLY

Universal Joints.

I designed and printed the six-way nodes, sphere-ended rods, thread clips and bracing bars, then built them into a frame. I kept adjusting the parts through repeated print trials. The rods can pivot at the nodes; the thread works in tension and the clip-ended bars in compression.

Designed + built3D printingStructural system
01 / BUILT SYSTEM

From a joint to a frame.

I assembled the kit in different configurations. The white rods and nodes make the frame; orange thread and gray clip-ended bars brace selected parts of it.

02 / ASSEMBLY STUDY

Make the connection.

Orbit the frame, place a node, seat a sphere-ended member, then add an orange thread tie or a gray two-grip bar at node edges. The four printed parts are taken from the saved Rhino model.

VIEW

BUILD WITH THE PRINTED PARTS

Choose a part, click a node to reveal its contacts, then build the connection. Drag to orbit; scroll or pinch to zoom.

This is an interactive assembly study using the saved part meshes. The angle control illustrates the joints’ movement; it is not a force simulation or a verified fit test.

03 / CONNECTION

Three kinds of contact.

Six bowl sockets turn the node outward in every direction. A sphere at each end of the main rod sits in a bowl and lets the rod change angle. Small clips grip the designated node edges: their closed eyes carry the orange thread loops. The long gray bars use open C grips at both ends.

The connection took trials.

A printed joint needs to fit, release and hold in the hand, not just look right in a model. I printed it repeatedly, changed settings and made small adjustments before assembling the larger frame.

University field trip / photographs / Shiraz & Fars

Shiraz, and fallen empires.

My first university field trip, with Professor Memarian and my classmates. We went from Tehran to Shiraz by train and returned the same way. Between walks through the city and long days among the ruins of Fars, architecture became something we could enter, climb and look at together.

Explore ↓
01 / City & gardens

Shiraz, on foot.

We walked through the historic city, visited Hafez and Saadi, and spent time in Eram Garden and the Arg of Karim Khan. The scale kept changing: a long avenue of cypresses, the shade of a courtyard, a windcatcher above the trees. I helped coordinate the trip with a team, but what I remember most is being there with my classmates.

02 / Nasir al-Mulk

The Pink Mosque, from courtyard to ceiling.

At Nasir al-Mulk, I kept looking up. The photographs move from the courtyard and its reflection to the vaults, tiled surfaces and muqarnas overhead. Seen together, the larger spaces and the closely framed details show how many different geometries occupy the same building.

03 / The old quarter

Behind the street walls.

Historic houses opened into rooms of coloured glass, painting and mirrorwork. Elsewhere, we came across a mosque in the old quarter that I remember as abandoned. These walks stay with me alongside the larger monuments. Knowing how much of the historic fabric around Shah Cheragh has been lost gives these photographs another weight.

04 / Firuzabad

Qal‘eh Dokhtar, then Ardashir.

One long day by bus began near Firuzabad. First came Qal‘eh Dokhtar, high above the valley; then the Palace of Ardashir, with its great vaulted entrance facing the spring-fed pool. Inside, the walls, niches and surviving dome made the construction much easier to understand than a plan alone.

05 / Bishapur

A city, and a room for water.

From Firuzabad we continued to Bishapur. The sunken stone chamber is generally known as the Temple of Anahita, associated with the water deity. Around it, the city survives in fragments of walls and columns. By sunset, a pair of columns stood against the last orange light.

06 / Persepolis & Pasargadae

Another day, another empire.

A separate outing took us to Persepolis and Pasargadae. At Persepolis, we looked across the terrace and between the columns, then down at the details of the stone joints. At Pasargadae, the Tomb of Cyrus changed character as the light faded: dark stone beneath the clouds, then a small illuminated presence at the end of the path.

07 / The bus journey

The landscape between stops.

These are photographs from the bus excursion around Fars. Plains, mountain roads, water and passing ruins filled the intervals between visits. We returned by a different route, through the landscape around Dasht-e Arzhan. I remember the gorge, though I can no longer confidently name it.

08 / Between visits

The rest of the trip.

There was faloodeh Shirazi, meals together, and evenings back at the dorm playing games. I no longer remember the restaurants’ names, but I kept photographs of their rooms and ceilings. It was a course trip with Professor Memarian, and also the first time I had travelled this way with my university classmates.

Two journeys / photographs + notebook / Winter & spring 2025

Kurdistan, in two seasons.

I visited Bijar twice for the same design studio, a carpet museum for the city. In winter I went alone by bus to see the site and meet people. In spring I returned with the university, travelling through Hawraman/Uramanat and Marivan before passing through Bijar again. The two trips became part of my thinking for the museum.

Explore ↓
01 / Bijar / winter

Alone in the cold.

In February I took a two-day solo trip to Bijar to see the land for our museum project. Snow covered the hills and fields. Walking there, I felt how severe the winter could be; later, the same region’s spring greenery would be almost difficult to reconcile with it. The surviving photographs from Bijar date to 26 to 27 February 2025.

02 / People & memory

A house drawn from memory.

People made room for a stranger. One older man invited me home for a local ash soup; in the old bazaar, I was offered tea beneath a korsi, the low table covered with blankets. The man whose home I visited drew the plan of his former house in my notebook. I drew him while he described Bijar as it had been when he was young. These pages belong to that winter visit.

03 / Carpet making

Before the finished carpet.

Those conversations also opened a way into carpet workshops. I watched weaving at the loom and saw the yarn and dyeing process. A carpet’s softness and beauty conceal a great deal of difficult work. That contrast became part of how I thought about the museum: the finished object alongside the materials, effort and lives that make it possible.

04 / Spring journey

Back with the university.

We took the train to Sanandaj, beyond Hamedan, then continued by bus to Hawraman/Uramanat, where we stayed. This trip was mostly spent outdoors, in green fields and on mountain roads. The camera photographs are dated 1 and 2 May 2025. The small notebook page below is from this second trip.

05 / Villages & slopes

As though it had grown from the ground.

From the winding mountain road, the villages seemed to rise out of the same earth as the slopes around them. Their stone, scale and placement felt like part of the landscape. I remember thinking how different that was from cities that seem to have been placed over nature. The feeling was organic, even where construction and road work interrupted the view.

06 / Marivan / lake

Zarivar, beside Marivan.

We also visited Marivan and Zarivar Lake, often called Zeribar. The broad water and reed beds here are a different setting from Daryan reservoir on the Sirwan River, where we took a boat ride near Uramanat. I remember both, but they belong to separate stops.

07 / Sirwan / boat

Between the banks.

On the boat at Daryan reservoir, on the Sirwan River near Uramanat, the mountains came close to the water. Looking from that level changed the scale again: steep green banks, rock faces and another small boat across the surface. These photographs were made on and around that ride.

08 / Small encounters

Sharing the landscape.

We stopped to watch the storks and photograph their nests. I also kept pictures of a tortoise in the grass, a spider on the ground and a cow resting above the road. These small encounters are part of how I remember the spring trip.

09 / Bijar / return

Back through Bijar.

Coming back through Bijar in spring, I remembered the hills covered in snow on my first visit. The change in season was striking. The people I had met were equally memorable. They lived with a hard winter, yet welcomed me into their homes and shops. In the museum studio I kept returning to those experiences, and to the contrast between a soft, beautiful carpet and the hard work needed to make it.
